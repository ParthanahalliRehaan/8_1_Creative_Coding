## 1. NOTES

### 🔤 GDScript
- **`AnimatedSprite2D.play("name")`**: starts the named animation. The name must match the SpriteFrames panel exactly, capitalization included.
- **Calling `play()` every frame is safe** if the animation is already playing, because Godot won't restart it. That's why mapping state → animation each frame works for looping animations.
- **One-shot animations are different**: a kick must not loop. Look up how `animation_finished` works, and think about who owns "kick is over": the animation ending, or your state machine's timer?
- **`flip_h`**: mirrors the sprite. Draw one facing direction only.
- **`match` on your state enum**: one line per state picks the animation name. The state machine is the single source of truth.
- **Signals Godot emits for you**: `animation_finished` and `frame_changed`. Same mechanism as your custom signals on Day 7, but Godot is the emitter.

### 🎮 Godot
- **`Sprite2D` vs `AnimatedSprite2D`**: one image vs a frame sequence.
- **`SpriteFrames` resource**: where you define named animations, their frames, FPS, and Loop flag.
- **`AnimationPlayer` (awareness only)**: a more powerful tool that can animate *any* property over time (sprite frame, hitbox enabled, color, sound). Today you only learn what it is. It becomes important when a kick's hitbox must switch on at exactly frame 2.
- **Texture Filter → Nearest**: Project Settings → Rendering → Textures → Default Texture Filter. Without it, pixel art goes blurry.
- **Stretch settings**: Project Settings → Display → Window → Stretch. Compare `canvas_items` and `viewport` modes, and look up what "integer scaling" does.
- **Collision shapes are separate from art**: swapping the ColorRect for a sprite does not resize your hitbox.
- **TileSet / TileMapLayer (awareness only)**: a tile is a reusable square image, a TileSet is the collection, a TileMapLayer paints the level. Day 14 builds with it.

### 🎨 Art basics
- **Pixel art**: every pixel is deliberate. Small canvases are easier, not harder.
- **Palette**: 8 to 12 colors for the whole game. Fastest way to look cohesive.
- **Silhouette test**: fill the sprite solid black. Is it still clearly an ostrich?
- **Why a walk cycle reads well**: look up **contact pose** (feet at furthest apart) and **passing pose** (legs crossing under the body). A 4-frame walk is just contact, passing, contact (other leg), passing.
- **Weight and bob**: the body dips slightly when the foot lands and rises at the passing pose. That 1-pixel bob is what makes a walk feel alive.
- **Squash and stretch (light touch)**: for the jump and plunge, a 1 to 2 pixel stretch on takeoff and squash on landing sells the motion.
- **Anticipation and follow-through**: a kick needs a small wind-up frame, the strike, and a recovery frame. Without a wind-up, the player can't read the attack.
- **Onion skinning**: Aseprite shows a ghost of neighboring frames so you can judge motion.
- **Tags**: name frame ranges (`idle`, `walk`, `kick`) in the timeline.

### 📐 Math
- **Spritesheet math**: width = columns × frame width. A 4×4 sheet of 32×32 frames is 128×128.
- **Powers of two (8, 16, 32, 64)**: why sizes cluster there. Connect it to the bitmask idea from Day 4.
- **Frame timing**: N frames at F FPS lasts N ÷ F seconds.
- **Integer scaling**: scale ×2, ×3, ×4 keeps every pixel a clean square. ×2.5 forces some pixels to be wider than others.
- **Time ownership**: your `plunge_timer` counts with `delta`. The animation has its own clock. Decide which one controls *gameplay*.

## 2. DOUBTS (think first, don't look up yet)

1. Your Ostrich is 32×32 and the window is 1152×648. Roughly how many Ostriches fit across the screen? Is that the right size?
2. An animation has 6 frames at 12 FPS. How long does it take once? What changes if it loops?
3. Why might a tiny canvas make pixel art easier for a beginner?
4. If you replace the ColorRect but never touch the collision shape, what could go wrong, and how would you notice?
5. Should the kick animation decide when the hitbox is active, or should your state logic decide? What breaks if they disagree?
6. Why draw only one facing direction and flip it?
7. Loop vs play-once: which of your states are which?
8. Where is the line between a tile and a prop? Is a cactus a tile?
9. Why might art look crisp in Aseprite but blurry in Godot?
10. Two players use the same Player scene. Do you draw two sets of sprites?
11. **(New)** If a kick animation finishes but your state is still `KICK`, what does the player see? What if the state ends first but the animation is mid-swing?
12. **(New)** At 32×32 on a 1152×648 window, what scale factor would you need to fill a clean pixel-art resolution like 384×216? Why is that number nice?

### Part D: Godot setup

18. Confirm the PNG shows up in FileSystem and looks crisp.

### Part E: Replace the rectangle
19. In `Player.tscn`, add an **`AnimatedSprite2D`** and keep the ColorRect temporarily for comparison.
20. Create a **SpriteFrames** resource. Make animations named to match your state enum (`idle`, `walk`, `kick`). Use "Add frames from Sprite Sheet".
21. Set FPS and Loop per animation using your answers to Doubts 2 and 7. **Idle and walk loop; kick does not.**
22. In `player.gd`, where state changes, have a `match` pick the animation name. **The state machine decides, the sprite follows.** Animation code must never change game behavior.
23. **Kick ownership:** decide who ends the kick state. Pick one approach (your timer, or the `animation_finished` signal), write your reasoning in 2 sentences (Doubt 11), and make sure the hitbox logic still lives in your state logic, not the animation.
24. Flip horizontally by movement direction. Decide what happens when velocity is zero (don't snap back to default).
25. Delete the ColorRect, then resize the **CollisionShape2D** to fit the sprite.
26. Run with **two players**. Each must animate independently. If one affects the other, something is shared that shouldn't be.
